# Overview Hydraulic Parameters

## 1. No freezing

### 1.1 Soil-retention curve $\theta_{tot}(h_c)$

van Genuchten (paper eq. (8)), with the capillary head $h_c\geq0$, the pore-size-distribution index $n\,[-]$ and $\alpha\,[m^{-1}]$, which is related to the inverse entry-pressure head:

$$
\theta_{tot}=\theta_r+(\theta_s-\theta_r)\left(1+(\alpha h_c)^n\right)^{\frac{1-n}{n}}
$$

$\theta_s$ is the saturated and $\theta_r$ the residual water content. The effective saturation of the total water is (paper eq. (10))

$$
S=\frac{\theta_{tot}-\theta_r}{\theta_s-\theta_r}.
$$

### 1.2 Relative hydraulic conductivity $k_{rel}$

van Genuchten-Mualem (paper eq. (9)):

$$
k_{rel}(S)=\sqrt{S}\left(1-\left(1-S^\frac{n}{n-1}\right)^\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^2
$$

## 2. With freezing

When the pore water starts freezing, the total water content $\theta_{tot}(h_c)$ from the soil-retention curve is split into a liquid part $\theta_\ell$ and an ice part $\theta_i$, and the relative hydraulic conductivity becomes a function of capillary head and temperature, $k_{rel}(h_c,T)$. The density difference between ice and liquid water is neglected.

Five configurations of three parameterizations are compared (see the table in the README for the names used in the code):

| Label | Parameterization | Phase partitioning | $k_{rel}$ |
|-------|-----------------|--------------------|-----------|
| (A) | drying assumption | SFC, eq. (SFC) | $k_{rel}(S_\ell)$ |
| (B) | drying assumption with impedance factor, $\Omega=10$ | SFC, eq. (SFC) | $k_{rel}(S_\ell)\cdot\max(10^{-\Omega\theta_i},10^{-6})$ |
| (C) | impedance factor, $\Omega=10$ | SFC, eq. (SFC) | $k_{rel}(S)\cdot\max(10^{-\Omega\theta_i},10^{-6})$ |
| (D) | impedance factor, $\Omega=50$ | SFC, eq. (SFC) | $k_{rel}(S)\cdot\max(10^{-\Omega\theta_i},10^{-6})$ |
| (E) | capillary bundle model | Gibbs-Thomson | Watanabe & Flury [2], adapted to Mualem |

The labels follow the results figures of the publication (Figures 6, 7, B.1, B.2).

### 2.1 Drying Assumption

This approach treats the effect of soil freezing on $k_{rel}$ as equivalent to the effect of drying (Harlan, 1973). It requires that freezing starts from pores with large radius to pores with smaller radius. The liquid water content is calculated with the soil freezing curve (paper eq. (11))

$$
\theta_\ell=\begin{cases}\theta_r+(\theta_{tot}-\theta_r)\exp\left(-\left(\frac{T-T_f}{\omega}\right)^2\right) & T\leq T_f\\ \theta_{tot} & T>T_f\end{cases} \tag{SFC}
$$

where $\omega$ is a fitting parameter and $T_f$ the freezing temperature (McKenzie et al., 2007). In the code $T_f = 0\,°C$ and $\omega = 1\,°C$. The ice content is calculated from $\theta_{tot}$ and $\theta_\ell$ through (paper eq. (12))

$$
\theta_i=\theta_{tot}-\theta_\ell.
$$

The relative hydraulic conductivity is then calculated with $k_{rel}(S)$, section 1.2, where $S$ is replaced by the effective saturation of the liquid water (paper eq. (13))

$$
S_\ell=\frac{\theta_\ell-\theta_r}{\theta_s-\theta_r}.
$$

**Remark (Clausius-Clapeyron equation):** the soil freezing curve (SFC) is an empirical parameterization, which is not based on the Clausius-Clapeyron equation. The water content could be related to temperature with the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, which relates temperature to pressure under the assumption of thermal equilibrium. When neglecting the deformation of the soil due to freezing/thawing, the equation is given by

$$
\frac{\partial p_w}{\partial T}\approx \frac{H_f\rho_\ell}{T}
$$

with $T$ in Kelvin. Integrating with respect to $T$ from the melting temperature $T_m$ results in

$$
p_w = H_f\rho_\ell \ln\left(\frac{T}{T_m}\right)\Leftrightarrow h_w=\frac{H_f}{g}\ln\left(\frac{T}{T_m}\right).
$$

This relation is not used in the parameterizations (A)-(D). The capillary bundle model (E) uses the related Gibbs-Thomson equation (see 2.3).

### 2.2 Impedance Factor

The reduced connectivity of the liquid phase is accounted for by multiplying the relative hydraulic conductivity with an empirical impedance factor that decreases with increasing ice content (paper eq. (14); McKenzie et al., 2007; Orgogozo et al., 2023 [1]):

$$
k_{rel}^*=k_{rel}\,\max\left(10^{-6},\,10^{-\Omega\theta_i}\right)
$$

where $\Omega\,[-]$ is the impedance parameter and the lower limit of $10^{-6}$ acts as a numerical floor.

To obtain the ice content $\theta_i$, the same soil freezing curve (SFC) as in the drying assumption is applied. Following Orgogozo et al. (2023), the impedance factor is multiplied to $k_{rel}(S)$ evaluated at the total effective saturation $S$ (C, D). Alternatively, it is multiplied to $k_{rel}(S_\ell)$ evaluated at the effective saturation of liquid water $S_\ell$, resulting in a combination of the drying assumption and the impedance factor (B; Taylor and Luthin, 1978; Endrizzi et al., 2014). Literature values of $\Omega$ range from 5 to 50; $\Omega=10$ and $\Omega=50$ are applied to $k_{rel}(S)$, and $\Omega=10$ is applied to $k_{rel}(S_\ell)$.

### 2.3 Capillary Bundle Model [2]

The third parameterization resolves the geometry of the ice within individual pores. It extends the capillary bundle model underlying the van Genuchten-Mualem parameterization, where the soil is considered as a collection of capillaries with varying radii. Under non-frozen conditions (paper eqs. (15) and (16))

$$
k_{rel}(S)=S^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\int_0^S\left(\frac{1}{h_c(S_*)}\right)dS_*}{\int_0^1\left(\frac{1}{h_c(S_*)}\right)dS_*}\right)^2, \qquad h_c(S_*)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(S_*^{n/(1-n)}-1\right)^{\frac{1}{n}},
$$

whose analytical solution is the van Genuchten-Mualem equation of section 1.2. Each effective saturation corresponds to the largest water-filled pore radius (Young-Laplace equation, paper eq. (A.4))

$$
R(S_*)=\frac{2\sigma_{aw}}{\rho_\ell g\,h_c(S_*)}
$$

with the surface tension of water $\sigma_{aw}=0.0756\,N\,m^{-1}$.

For frozen soils, Watanabe and Flury (2008) adapted the capillary bundle model accounting for ice formation within the capillaries from the center outwards towards the pore wall. The radius of the ice core is (paper eq. (17))

$$
r_i(R,T)=\begin{cases}R-d(T) & \text{if } R-d(T)>r_{GT}(T)\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
$$

where $d$ is the thickness of the liquid water film between ice core and pore wall (paper eq. (18))

$$
d(T)=\left(-\frac{A}{6\pi\rho_iH_f}\left(\frac{T_m}{T_m-T}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}
$$

and $r_{GT}$ is the critical (minimum) radius of the ice core (Gibbs-Thomson, paper eq. (19))

$$
r_{GT}(T)=\frac{\sigma_{ls}}{\rho_iH_f}\left(\frac{T_m}{T_m-T}\right),
$$

with the Hamaker constant $A=-10^{-19.5}\,J$, the surface tension between liquid water and ice $\sigma_{ls}=0.029\,N\,m^{-1}$, the melting temperature $T_m=273.15\,K$ and $T$ in Kelvin. Pores of a radius $R\leq r_{GT}(T)+d(T)$ remain ice free, whereas liquid water in pores with a larger radius is restricted to a cylindrical film of thickness $d(T)$.

The saturation of the pore space with liquid water is (paper eq. (20))

$$
S_\ell=\int_0^S\left(1-\frac{r_i^2(R(S_*),T)}{R^2(S_*)}\right)dS_*,
$$

and the relative hydraulic conductivity for frozen soils is (paper eq. (21))

$$
k_{rel}(S_\ell,T)=S_\ell^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\int_0^S\left(S_*^{n/(1-n)}-1\right)^{-\frac{1}{n}}f(R(S_*),T)\,dS_*}{\int_0^1\left(S_*^{n/(1-n)}-1\right)^{-\frac{1}{n}}dS_*}\right)^2
$$

with the reduction factor (paper eq. (22))

$$
f(R,T)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1-\frac{r_i^4(R,T)}{R^4}+\frac{\left(1-\frac{r_i^2(R,T)}{R^2}\right)^2}{\ln(r_i(R,T)/R)}} & \text{if } r_i(R,T)>0\\ 1 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$

Note that Watanabe and Flury (2008) used the parameterization of Burdine (1953), while the parameterization of Mualem (1976) is used here to ensure consistency with ice-free conditions. The derivation of $f(R,T)$ and the comparison to the original model are given in Appendix A of the publication.

### 2.4 Lookup tables

Evaluating the parameterizations (in particular the integrals of the capillary bundle model) during the simulation would be too expensive. Therefore $\theta_\ell$ and $k_{rel}$ are precomputed once for each soil type on a grid of capillary heads $h_c$ (from $S\in(0,1)$) and temperatures $T$, and stored as 2D interpolators (`interpax.Interpolator2D`, monotonic interpolation). These lookup tables are saved under `input/interpolation/` and evaluated in each Newton-Raphson iteration. Since the interpolators are differentiable, the Jacobian can still be computed by automatic differentiation.
